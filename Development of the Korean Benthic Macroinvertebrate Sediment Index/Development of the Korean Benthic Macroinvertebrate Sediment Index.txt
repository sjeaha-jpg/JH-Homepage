Abstract 16 
Freshwater sediments accumulate organic matter, nutrients, metals, and fine particles and can 17 
influence benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages. However, biological tools for evaluating 18 
sediment-associated ecological condition remain limited in Korean freshw ater systems. This 19 
study developed the Korean Benthic Macroinvertebrate Sediment Index (KBSI) as a field -20 
based biological indicator using data from 80 sites and 320 site-survey sample units. Sediment 21 
variables, including porewater total ammonia nitrogen (T AN), acid-volatile sulfides (AVS), 22 
total organic carbon (TOC), the Metals Tier II Index, and mud fraction, were summarized using 23 
principal component analysis. PC1 represented an integrated sediment -condition gradient 24 
associated with organic enrichment, red ucing conditions, relative sediment -bound metal 25 
contamination, and fine-particle accumulation. PC1 scores from 309 sample units containing 26 
selected indicator taxa were divided into nine class intervals using a Weibull cumulative 27 
distribution function and Sturges’ rule. Taxon-specific tolerance values and indicator weights 28 
were derived from occurrence distributions along this gradient and used to calculate KBSI. 29 
KBSI showed significant negative relationships with TAN, AVS, TOC, the Metals Tier II 30 
Index, mud fraction, and PC1, indicating that lower index values were associated with more 31 
degraded sediment -associated field conditions. The relationship between observed and 32 
calculated KBSI values supported internal agreement within the development dataset but did 33 
not constitute independent validation. KBSI values were classified into five ecological 34 
condition classes from A to E for practical interpretation. KBSI is intended for biological 35 
assessment of depositional sediment habitats and should be interpreted together with sediment 36 
physicochemical and habitat information rather than as an independent chemical sediment -37 
quality standard or direct toxicological endpoint. 38

1. Introduction 42 
Sediment in rivers and lakes is a particulate matrix of gravel, sand, silt, clay, and 43 
organic matter that stores watershed -derived pollutants over extended periods and exchanges 44 
solutes with the water column as a dynamic reactive system (Chapman, 1990; Zhou et al., 2024). 45 
Settling and resuspension, sorption and desorption, and redox -mediated transformations 46 
continually modify sediment properties (El-Sharkawy et al., 2025; Hiki et al., 2023) . Where 47 
fine fractions dominate, contaminant accumulation and bioavailability increase and can 48 
restructure benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages (Relyea et al., 2012; Wharton et al., 2023). 49 
Therefore, single -time measurements of water -column concentrations do not adequately 50 
represent cumulative contamination or habitat alteration, underscoring the need for biological 51 
indicators that reflect sediment-associated ecological conditions. 52 
The Oligochaete Index of Sediment Bioindication (IOBS) uses oligochaete 53 
assemblage composition to evaluate sediment -associated biological condition in relation to 54 
sediment impairment (AFNOR, 2002; AFNOR, 2016), and the U.S. Equilibrium Partitioning 55 
Sediment Benchmarks (ESB) quantify linkages between sediment chemistry and biological 56 
effects (U.S.EPA, 2003). The Sediment Quality Triad (SQT) integr ates chemistry, toxicity 57 
testing, and community data to diagnose ecological degradation in contaminated sediments 58 
(Chapman, 1990). Stress-specific biotic indices have also been developed: the Fine Sediment 59 
Biotic Index (FSBI) encodes sensitivities to fine particles (Relyea et al., 2012) , and the 60 
Proportion of Sediment-sensitive Invertebrates (PSI) uses the share of sensitive taxa to evaluate 61 
restoration outcomes (Extence et al., 2010). 62 
In coastal and estuarine environments, the Benthic Response Index (BRI) and the 63 
AZTI Marine Biotic Index (AMBI) apply weighted -tolerance principles and have informed 64 
analogous approaches for fresh waters (Borja et al., 2000; Smith et al., 2001) . More recently, 65 

--- Page 5 ---

multivariate formulations—such as the Hellenic Lake Littoral Biotic Index (HeLLBI) and the 66 
Cumulative Biotic Index (CBI) —have related community structure to pollution gradients 67 
within explicit statistical frame works, while high -throughput metabarcoding is improving 68 
congruence with morphology-based indices (Lobo et al., 2017; Mavromati et al., 2021; Sudarso 69 
et al., 2026). 70 
In Korea, nationally standardized tools for water -column conditions —such as the 71 
Korean Benthic macroinvertebrate Index of Biotic Integrity (KB -IBI)—are established, but 72 
sediment-focused indicators remain comparatively underdeveloped (Jun et al., 2012) . A 73 
domestic prototype, the Benthic macroinvertebrate Sediment Index (BSI), estimated taxon 74 
tolerance values and indicator weights from relative frequencies in fr eshwater sediments and 75 
computed a weighted mean using a Sediment Quality Index that integrates total organic carbon 76 
(TOC), ammonia (NH ₃–N), and heavy metals (Kim et al., 2019) . Although BSI showed 77 
statistical associations with sediment impairment gradients, its generality was constrained by 78 
limited sample size and taxonomic uncertainty within Chironomidae, indicating the need for a 79 
tool calibrated with broader spatial coverage and refined taxonomy. 80 
To address this gap, we developed the Korean Benthic Macroinvertebrate Sediment 81 
Index (KBSI) as a field-based biological indicator of sediment-associated ecological condition 82 
in Korean freshwaters. Specifically, this study aimed to: (1) assemble a nationwi de dataset of 83 
co-located benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages and sediment physicochemical conditions 84 
from major Korean river basins and representative waterbody types; (2) define an integrated 85 
sediment environmental gradient using PCA of porewater total ammonia nitrogen, acid-volatile 86 
sulfides, total organic carbon, heavy metals, and the mud fraction; (3) derive taxon -specific 87 
tolerance values and indicator weights from macroinvertebrate occurrence patterns along the 88 
PC1-based sediment gradient; and (4) f ormulate and examine KBSI as a community -level 89 

--- Page 6 ---

index by relating index values to individual sediment variables, the PC1 sediment gradient, and 90 
observed–calculated index agreement. KBSI is therefore intended not as a stand -alone 91 
chemical or toxicological measure of sediment quality, but as a biological indicator of benthic 92 
assemblage responses to sediment-associated environmental conditions under field settings. 93

2. Materials and methods 95 
2.1 Study area and sampling design 96 
2.1.1 Site description  97 
This study was conducted at 80 freshwater sediment monitoring sites distributed across 98 
the major river basins of Korea (Fig. 1). The sites were selected to represent broad gradients in 99 
sedimentary environments, waterbody type, and anthropogenic influence, including both lotic 100 
and lentic systems. For descriptive and management -oriented interpretation, the sites were 101 
categorized into five groups: general streams, urban streams, industrial streams, lakes, and 102 
reservoirs. The stream categories were assigned according to t he dominant surrounding land-103 
use setting and the original monitoring-site designation, rather than using uniform quantitative 104 
catchment-scale land -use thresholds. Urban streams represented lotic sites located in 105 
predominantly urbanized settings, whereas industrial streams represented sites associated with 106 
industrial areas. General streams comprised the remaining lotic sites that were not classified as 107 
urban or industrial streams. 108 
These categories were used to describe broad differences in waterbody type, 109 
surrounding land -use setting, and sediment depositional environment, rather than to define 110 
strict reference or impairment classes. General streams were therefore not treated as reference 111 
or best -available sites and could include streams influenced by agricul ture, forestry, flow 112 

--- Page 7 ---

regulation, impoundments, or other catchment and hydromorphological pressures. 113 
Field surveys were conducted repeatedly between 2022 and 2024, producing a total of 114 
320 site-survey sample units. For the derivation of taxon-specific tolerance values and indicator 115 
weights, 309 sample units in which selected indicator taxa occurred were used. Sample units 116 
without selected indicator taxa were excluded from the tolerance -value calculation because 117 
they did not provide the taxon-occurrence information required to estimate biological responses 118 
along the sediment environmental gradient. 119 
The sampling design was developed to support a field-based biological assessment of 120 
sediment-associated ecological condition. Accordingly, biological and sediment samples were 121 
collected from comparable depositional habitats within each site, where fine sed iments and 122 
associated organic or reduced materials could accumulate. The sampling framework was 123 
therefore intended for depositional sediment habitats, not for coarse riffle or boulder-dominated 124 
substrates. This design allowed the KBSI to characterize benthic macroinvertebrate assemblage 125 
responses to sediment-associated environmental conditions under field settings. 126 
 127 
2.1.2. Collection and Identification of Benthic Macroinvertebrates 128 
Benthic macroinvertebrates were collected from depositional sediment habitat s using 129 
a cylindrical core sampler with an internal diameter of 7.5 cm. At each site, five replicate cores 130 
were taken to a depth of 20 cm where coring was feasible (Kim et al., 2018; Song et al., 2025). 131 
The 20 cm sampling depth was adopted to capture not only organisms concentrated in the upper 132 
sediment layer, but also burrowing taxa, particularly oligochaetes, that may occur in deeper 133 
sediment layers. 134 
In the field, sediment core contents were washed through a 0.20-mm sieve to minimize 135 

--- Page 8 ---

the loss of small -bodied sediment-dwelling organisms, particularly oligochaetes and 136 
chironomid larvae. Retained materials were preserved in ethanol and transported to the 137 
laboratory. The use of a 0.20 -mm sieve was selected because small -bodied taxa in fine 138 
sediments can be underestimated when larger mesh sizes are used (Song et al., 2025). 139 
In the laboratory, residual sediment was removed and organisms were sorted under a 140 
stereomicroscope. Specimens were identified to the lowest practicable taxonomic level, 141 
generally to species wher e diagnostic characters were available, using standard taxonomic 142 
references (Cummins and Merritt, 1996; Kawai and Tanida, 2005; Peckarsky et al., 1990; Yoon, 143 
1988). Chironomid larvae were identified based on diagnostic structures such as the head 144 
capsule, mentum, mandible, and antennae (Kwak, 2015). Oligochaetes were identified using 145 
external and reproductive m orphological characters, including chaetal and reproductive 146 
structures (Park et al., 2016) . All identified specimens were preserved in  80% ethanol to 147 
minimize morphological deformation.  148 
Taxa that were sufficiently frequent and taxonomically reliable were retained as 149 
candidate indicator taxa for KBSI development. This taxonomic screening was necessary 150 
because the derivation of tolerance values and indicator weights requires reliable occurrence 151 
information along the sediment environmental gradient. 152 
 153 
2.1.3. Sediment Sampling and Physicochemical Analysis 154 
Surface sediment samples for physicochemical analysis were collected concurrently 155 
with the biological samples from the corresponding depositional habitats within each site using 156 
a cylindrical core sampler with an internal diameter of 7.5 cm.  For sediment chemistry and 157 
particle-size analysis, the upper 0–7 cm sediment layer was used because this layer represents 158 

--- Page 9 ---

the biologically active surface sediment where organic matter accumulation, redox gradients, 159 
porewater chemistry, and contaminant binding are most pronounced. This analytical depth 160 
differed from the macroinvertebrate sampling depth of 20 cm, which was used to include 161 
deeper-burrowing taxa. 162 
Each sediment sample was sealed in a 300-mL high-density polyethylene bottle, stored 163 
at ≤4 °C, and transported to the laboratory. The target sediment variables were porewater total 164 
ammonia nitrogen (T AN), acid -volatile sulfides (A VS), total organic carbon (TOC), heavy 165 
metals, and particle -size composition. TAN was used as an indicator of nitrogenous organic 166 
enrichment in sediment porewater, A VS represented reduced sediment conditions associated 167 
with sulfide formation, and TOC represented organic matter accumulation. 168 
TAN was quantified colorimetrically at 630 nm. TOC was measured using a CHNS 169 
analyzer after removal of inorganic carbon. A VS was determined as Ag₂S precipitated under a 170 
nitrogen stream, foll owed by filtration and gravimetric drying. For heavy metals, sediment 171 
samples were digested with mixed acids and analyzed using ICP –AES or ICP –MS; Hg was 172 
quantified using TDA–AAS. Relative sediment-bound metal contamination was summarized 173 
using the Metals Tier II Index based on the Tier II sediment guideline values for Cu, Pb, Ni, 174 
As, Hg, Zn, Cd, and Cr (NIER, 2022).  175

2.2. Development and calculation of the Korean Benthic Macroinvertebrate Sediment Index 189 
(KBSI) 190 
2.2.1. PCA-derived sediment environmental gradient 191 
To derive an integrated sediment environmental gradient for KBSI development, 192 
principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the sediment physicochemical 193 
variables measured at each sample unit. PCA was used to reduce redundancy among correlated 194 
environmental va riables and to summarize the dominant multivariate gradient in sediment 195 
condition (Abdi and Williams, 2010; Jolliffe and Cadima, 2016) . The variables included 196 
porewater total ammonia nitrogen (TAN), acid -volatile sulfides (A VS), total organic carbon 197 
(TOC), the Metals Tier II Index, and the mud fraction . These variables were selected to 198 
represent major sediment -associated conditions, including nitrogenous organic enrichment, 199 
reduced sediment conditions, organic matter accumulation, relative sediment -bound metal 200 
contamination, and fine-particle deposition. 201 
Prior to PCA, variables with skewed distributions were transformed to reduce the 202 
influence of extreme values. TAN, A VS, TOC, and the Metals Tier II Index were log -203 
transformed, whereas the mud fraction, expressed as proportional data, was arcsine square-root 204 

--- Page 11 ---

transformed. All transformed variables were standardized to a mean of zero and a standard 205 
deviation of one before analysis, and PCA was conducted on the standardized environmental 206 
matrix (Legendre and Legendre, 2012; Quinn and Keough, 2002). 207 
The first principal component score was used as the integrated sediment environmental 208 
gradient for deriving taxon-specific tolerance values and indicator weights. PC1 was selected 209 
because it represented the dominant axis  of variation in sediment conditions and provided a 210 
continuous gradient along which macroinvertebrate occurrence patterns could be evaluated. In 211 
this study, PC1 was not interpreted as a stand-alone chemical pollution index or an independent 212 
measure of sediment toxicity. Instead, it was used as a multivariate sediment-condition gradient 213 
reflecting field-based co-variation among sediment physicochemical variables. 214 
The PC1 scores of sample units containing selected indicator taxa were used in the 215 
subsequent derivation of biological indicator values. Among the 320 site-survey sample units, 216 
309 sample units with selected indicator taxa were retained for this step, whereas sample units 217 
without selected indicator taxa were excluded because they did not provide occu rrence 218 
information required for estimating taxon responses along the sediment environmental gradient. 219

3. Results and discussion 323 
3.1. PCA-derived sediment environmental gradient 324 
Principal component analysis summarized the covariance structure of the five 325 
sediment physicochemical variables used to characterize sediment-associated field conditions: 326 
porewater total ammonia nitrogen (TAN), acid -volatile sulfides (A VS), total organic carbon 327 
(TOC), Metals Tier II Index, and mud fraction. The first three PCA axes explained 86.0% of 328 
the total variance, with PC1 accounting for 58.0%, PC2 for 17.2%, and PC3 for 10.8% (Table 329 
1; Fig. 2). PC1 was the dominant axis because all five sediment vari ables showed clear 330 
associations with this axis. The loadings on PC1 were 0.66 for ln(TAN), 0.69 for ln(A VS), 0.86 331 
for ln(TOC), 0.82 for ln(Metals Tier II Index), and 0.76 for ln(mud fraction).  Thus, PC1 332 
represented a composite sediment environmental gradient along which nitrogenous enrichment, 333 
reducing sediment conditions, organic matter accumulation, relative sediment -bound metal 334 
contamination, and fine-particle deposition co-varied. 335 
Organic matter accumulation is ecologically relevant because it can incre ase oxygen 336 
demand in surface sediments and promote conditions unfavorable to oxygen-sensitive benthic 337 
fauna (Rosenberg et al., 2001). A VS reflects reduced sediment conditions, and sulfide-rich or 338 
strongly reduced sediments can  modify metal binding, porewater chemistry, and exposure 339 

--- Page 17 ---

conditions for sediment-dwelling organisms (Simpson et al., 2013). TAN was interpreted as a 340 
porewater indicator of nitrogenous organic enrichment rather than as a stand -alone toxicant 341 
gradient, because ammonia effects in sediments depend on pH, redox condition, and 342 
porewater–overlying water exchange. The Metals Tier II Index summarized the relative degree 343 
of sediment -bound metal contamination, but its ecological interpretation should consider 344 
bioavailability and site -specific geochemical conditions rather than total concentration alon e 345 
(Simpson et al., 2013). Therefore, the strong contribution of TOC, A VS, TAN, and the Metals 346 
Tier II Index indicates that PC1 captured a biogeochemical gradient associated with organic 347 
enrichment, reduced sediment conditions, and relative sediment-bound metal contamination. 348 
Mud fraction also contributed strongly to PC1, but its interpretation differs from that 349 
of chemical contaminants. Fine particles can reduce interstitial flow, alter oxygen exchange, 350 
and modify benthic habitat structure in lotic environments (Wood and Armitage, 1997). Fine-351 
grained sediment can also affect macroinvertebrate assemblages by filling substrate interstices 352 
and reducing the availability of coarse-substrate microhabitats used by rheophilic or sediment-353 
sensitive taxa (Bylak and Kukuła, 2022). In addition, fine sediments can enhance the retention 354 
of organic matter and particle -reactive contaminants because of their large surface area and 355 
depositional stability (Wharton et al., 2023) . Howeve r, mud accumulation may arise from 356 
natural hydromorphological and depositional processes as well as from anthropogenic changes 357 
in flow regulation, land use, and sediment supply (Wharton et al., 2023; Wood and Armitage, 358 
1997). Accordingly, mud fraction was treated as a physical depositional variable rather than as 359 
a chemical contaminant. 360 
For these reasons, PC1 was interpreted as a field -based multivariate sediment -361 
condition gradient, not as a stand -alone sediment toxicity index or a regulatory sediment -362 
quality standard. Sediment chemistry and benthic community composition can provide 363 

--- Page 18 ---

complementary lines of evidence for sediment -associated ecological assessment, but neither 364 
line of evidence alone fully resolves causal mechanisms under field conditions (Long and 365 
Chapman, 1985). The sediment quality triad framework similarly emphasizes that sediment 366 
chemistry, toxicity, and benthic community responses should be interpreted together rather than 367 
treated as interchangeable measures (Chapman, 1990). In the present study, KBSI was derived 368 
from macroinvertebrate occurrence patterns along the PC1 gradient; therefore, it should be 369 
interpreted as a biological indicator of sediment -associated ecological c ondition under field 370 
settings, not as an independent chemical or toxicological assessment of sediment quality. 371

3.2. Classification of PC1 scores and derivation of class values 373 
Among the 320 site-survey sample units, 309 sample units contained selected indicator 374 
taxa and were therefore used to classify the PC1 sediment -condition gradient. The remaining 375 
11 sample units were excluded from this step because they did not contain selected indicator 376 
taxa and therefore provided no taxon -occurrence information for deriving tolerance values or 377 
indicator weights. The PC1 scores of the 309 retained sample units were used to describe the 378 
empirical distribution of sediment -associated field conditions represented by the indicator -379 
taxon dataset. 380 
The distribution of PC1 scores was fitted using a Weibull cumulative distribution 381 
function (Fig. 3). The Weibull model was used to describe the probability distribution of PC1 382 
scores and to derive class boundaries along the continuous sediment -condition gradient. 383 
Because the objective was to construct empirical intervals for biological indicator derivation 384 
rather than to define regulatory sediment -quality thresholds, the distribution was divided into 385 
equal cumulative-probability intervals. Sturges’ rule produced nine class intervals for the 309 386 
sample units, and the inverse Weibull cumulative distribution function was used to estimate the 387 

--- Page 19 ---

PC1 boundary and representative class value for each interval (Table 2). 388 
The resulting PC1 classes covered the observed sediment-condition gradient from low 389 
PC1 scores to high PC1 scores. Under the sign convention used in this study, higher PC1 scores 390 
represented stronger sediment -associated environmental stress, including grea ter organic 391 
enrichment, reducing conditions, relative sediment -bound metal contamination, and mud 392 
accumulation. The representative class values therefore provided a standardized numerical 393 
basis for estimating taxon-specific occurrence positions along the sediment-condition gradient. 394 
Taxa occurring mainly in lower PC1 classes were assigned lower tolerance values, whereas 395 
taxa occurring mainly in higher PC1 classes were assigned higher tolerance values. 396 
Importantly, these PC1 classes should not be interpreted as sediment-quality standards 397 
or toxicity thresholds. They are empirical intervals of a field -based multivariate sediment -398 
condition gradient derived from the distribution of PC1 scores in sample units containing 399 
indicator taxa. This distinction is necessa ry because PC1 integrates chemical variables with 400 
mud fraction, a physical depositional variable that can reflect both natural hydromorphological 401 
processes and anthropogenic sediment accumulation. The class values were therefore used only 402 
as an internal scaling framework for deriving KBSI tolerance values and indicator weights, not 403 
as independent criteria for classifying sediment contamination or toxicity. 404

3.3. Taxon-specific tolerance values and indicator weights 406 
Taxon-specific tolerance values and indica tor weights were derived from the 407 
occurrence distributions of selected indicator taxa across the nine PC1 class intervals (Table 3). 408 
In total, 137 indicator taxa were retained in the final indicator table. This approach follows the 409 
logic of field-based macroinvertebrate bioassessment, in which taxon scores are inferred from 410 

--- Page 20 ---

observed distributions along environmental gradients rather than from a single chemical 411 
variable or laboratory endpoint (Hering et al., 2004; Rosenberg and Resh, 1993). It also builds 412 
on the saprobic valency concept and its later adaptations, where taxa are characterized by both 413 
their position along an environmental gradient and their strength of indication within that 414 
gradient (Sládeček, 1967; Yoon et al., 1992; Zelinka and Marvan, 1961). 415 
Although KBSI shares a weighted -indicator structure with the Korean BMI  (Kong et 416 
al., 2018) and conventional saprobic indices, the indices differ in their target environmental  417 
gradients and application domains. BMI is used primarily to assess the general ecological 418 
condition of Korean streams, whereas KBSI was derived from depositional sediment habitats 419 
in both lotic and lentic systems. Similarly, conventional saprobic values p rimarily represent 420 
taxon distributions along gradients of organic pollution and oxygen conditions, whereas KBSI 421 
tolerance values were independently derived along a multivariate sediment-condition gradient 422 
integrating TAN, A VS, TOC, relative metal contamination, and mud fraction. Correspondence 423 
among taxon classifications may therefore reflect shared responses to organic enrichment and 424 
oxygen depletion, but KBSI tolerance values should not be interpreted as conventional saprobic 425 
values. 426 
Tolerance values represent the weighted average position of each taxon along the PC1 427 
sediment-condition gradient. Lower values indicate taxa whose occurrences were concentrated 428 
in lower PC1 classes, whereas higher values indicate taxa whose occurrences were 429 
concentrated in higher PC1 classes. Under the sign convention used in this study, higher PC1 430 
scores represented stronger sediment -associated environmental stress, including greater 431 
organic enrichment, reducing conditions, metal contamination, and mud accumulation. 432 
Accordingly, tolerance values should be interpreted as field-derived occurrence positions along 433 
the measured PC1 gradient, not as fixed physiological toxicity thresholds. 434 

--- Page 21 ---

The resulting values showed broad taxon -level variation. Taxa with low tolerance 435 
values included Rhyacodrilus coccineus (t = 0.0), Chaetogaster limnaei (t = 0.0), Limnodrilus 436 
claparedeianus (t = 0.7), and Chaetogaster diaphanus  (t = 1.9), whereas taxa with  higher 437 
tolerance values included Limnodrilus udekemianus (t = 7.3) and Branchiodrilus hortensis (t = 438 
7.1). These differences are consistent with the general observation that benthic 439 
macroinvertebrate assemblages vary along gradients of organic enrichment, oxygen depletion, 440 
sediment condition, and habitat disturbance (Townsend et al., 2008; V on Bertrab et al., 2013). 441 
However, the values should not be over -interpreted as universal taxon traits because field 442 
distributions can also reflect habitat structure, flow regime, sampling depth, and regional 443 
species pools. 444 
Indicator weights describe a different property from tolerance values. A high indicator 445 
weight means that a taxon ’s occurrences were concentrated within a restricted portion of the 446 
PC1 gradient, whereas a low weight indicates a broader distribution across classes. Thus, 447 
indicator weight represents occurrence specificity r ather than sensitivity or tolerance itself. 448 
This distinction follows the weighted -indicator logic of saprobic and Korean benthic 449 
macroinvertebrate indices, where community-level scores combine taxon position, abundance 450 
or occurrence, and indicator strength  (Zelinka & Marvan, 1961; Yoon et al., 1992; Kong & 451 
Kim, 2016; Kim et al., 2019). 452 
This distinction also resolves the issue of taxa with tolerance values of 0.0. In the final 453 
table, Rhyacodrilus coccineus  and Chaetogaster limnaei  had tolerance values of 0.0  and 454 
indicator weights of 5, because their occurrences were concentrated in the lowest PC1 class. 455 
Their high indicator weights therefore reflect narrow occurrence specificity, not an automatic 456 
assignment based on sensitivity. Conversely, taxa with intermed iate or high tolerance values 457 
may receive low weights if they are broadly distributed across PC1 classes. This treatment 458 

--- Page 22 ---

prevents tolerance value and indicator weight from being conflated into a single sensitivity 459 
metric. 460 
Overall, Table 3 should be interpreted as a field-derived indicator table for sediment -461 
associated ecological condition in the sampled Korean freshwater systems. The table 462 
summarizes how selected taxa were distributed along the measured PC1 gradient, but it does 463 
not imply that KBSI fully is olates sediment effects from water -column conditions or habitat 464 
context. This cautious interpretation is consistent with macroinvertebrate bioassessment 465 
frameworks, which recognize that benthic taxa integrate multiple stressors and habitat -scale 466 
processes rather than responding to a single environmental factor alone (Birk et al., 2012; 467 
Hering et al., 2004; Rosenberg and Resh, 1993) . The use of taxon -specific tolerance values 468 
requires standardized identification at the lowest practicable taxonomic level and may limit 469 
routine application where specialist taxonomic expertise is unavailable. Future operational 470 
evaluation should determine whether selected taxa can be aggregated to hig her taxonomic 471 
levels without materially reducing the discriminatory performance of KBSI. 472

3.4. Relationship between KBSI and sediment environmental variables 474 
Using site -level annual mean values for the 80 monitoring sites, KBSI showed 475 
consistent negative relationships with the five sediment environmental variables used to define 476 
the PC1 sediment-condition gradient (Fig. 4). KBSI decreased with increasing porewater TAN, 477 
A VS, TOC, Metals Tier II Index, and mud fraction, and all relationships were statistical ly 478 
significant. At the site level, the correlation coefficients were −0.57 for TAN, −0.60 for A VS, 479 
−0.65 for TOC, −0.64 for the Metals Tier II Index, and −0.51 for mud fraction.  Thus, the 480 
strongest associations were observed for TOC and Metals Tier II Index, followed by A VS, TAN, 481 
and mud fraction. These results indicate that lower KBSI values were associated with 482 

--- Page 23 ---

depositional sediments characterized by greater organic matter content, greater relative 483 
sediment-bound metal contamination, stronger reducing conditions, higher porewater nitrogen 484 
enrichment, and greater fine-particle accumulation. 485 
The negative relationship between KBSI and TOC suggests that the index responded 486 
to organic matter accumulation in surface sediments. Organic matter enrichment can incre ase 487 
microbial oxygen demand and promote low-oxygen or reducing conditions in benthic habitats, 488 
thereby affecting sediment-dwelling fauna and benthic community composition (Eggleton and 489 
Thomas, 2004; Rosenberg et al., 2001) . TOC can also interact with redox condition and 490 
contaminant binding, so its relationship with KBSI should be interpreted as part of a broader 491 
sediment biogeochemical gradient rather than as an isolated single-stressor effect (Zhang et al., 492 
2014). 493 
KBSI also decreased with increasing TAN. In this study, TAN was interpreted as a 494 
porewater indicator of nitrogenous organic enrichment rather than as a stand -alone toxicant 495 
gradient. Ammonia and other inorganic nitrogen forms can affect aquatic organisms, but their 496 
effects depend on chemical speciation, pH, temperature, oxygen condition, and exchange 497 
between porewater and overlying water (Camargo and Alonso, 2006; U.S. EPA, 2013) . 498 
Therefore, the KBSI –TAN relationship is best interpreted as a field -level association with 499 
nitrogenous enrichment and porewater condition within depositional sediments. 500 
The negative relationship bet ween KBSI and A VS indicates that lower index values 501 
were associated with more reduced sediment conditions. A VS is important in sediment 502 
assessment because sulfide-rich conditions reflect reducing environments and can modify both 503 
porewater chemistry and met al partitioning (Di Toro et al., 1992) . However, A VS should not 504 
be interpreted simply as a direct stressor with a single directional effect, because sulfides can 505 
bind divalent metals and reduce their bioavailabi lity under some geochemical conditions (Di 506 

--- Page 24 ---

Toro et al., 1992; Zhang et al., 2014) . Accordingly, the KBSI –A VS relationship should be 507 
interpreted as a biological response associated with reduced, organic -rich depositional 508 
conditions rather than as direct evidence of sulfide toxicity. 509 
KBSI decreased with increasing Metals Tier II Index, suggesting that assemblages with 510 
lower KBSI values tended to occur where relative sediment -bound metal contamination was 511 
higher. Sediments can act as both sinks and secondary sources of metals, and metal mobility 512 
and bioavailability are influenced by particle size, organic matter, Fe –Mn oxides, A VS, pH, 513 
redox condition, and biological activity (Eggleton and Thomas, 2004; Förstner and Wittmann, 514 
1981). Therefore, the relationship between KBSI and the Metals Tier II Index should be 515 
interpreted as a field -level association between benthic assemblage condition and relative 516 
sediment-bound metal contamination, not as a direct measurement of metal toxicity.  This 517 
distinction is important because total or guideline -normalized metal concentrations do not 518 
necessarily represent the fraction that is bioavailable to benthic organisms (Di Toro et al., 1992; 519 
Zhang et al., 2014). 520 
KBSI also showed a statistically significant but moderate negative relationship with 521 
mud fraction, indicating a general tendency for lower KBSI values to occur in  depositional 522 
sediments with a greater silt–clay proportion. However, mud fraction alone accounted for only 523 
a limited proportion of the variation in KBSI and should not be interpreted as the dominant 524 
determinant of index values. Fine sediments can alter be nthic habitats by reducing interstitial 525 
flow, filling substrate spaces, changing oxygen exchange, and increasing the retention of 526 
organic matter and particle -reactive contaminants (V on Bertrab et al., 2013; Wood and 527 
Armitage, 1997). Fine-sediment deposition can also influence macroinvertebrate assemblage 528 
composition and interact with other stressors such as organic enrichment and water -quality 529 
degradation (Doretto et al., 2018; Extence et al., 2010) . Nevertheless, mud fraction is not a 530 

--- Page 25 ---

chemical contaminant. It can reflect natural depositional and hydromorphological processes as 531 
well as anthropogenic changes in flow regulation, sediment supply, land use,  or channel 532 
modification (Wharton et al., 2023; Wood and Armitage, 1997). 533 
Overall, these relationships support the internal consistency of KBSI with the sediment 534 
variables used to define the PC1 gradient. They indicate that KBSI varied consistently along 535 
field gradients involving organic enrichment, reducing conditions, relative sediment -bound 536 
metal contamination, and fine -particle deposition. However, these associations do not 537 
demonstrate that KBSI isolates sediment effects from co-varying water-column conditions or 538 
habitat characteristics (Birk et al., 2012; Feld and Hering, 2007; Townsend et al., 2008). 539

3.5. Relationship between PC1 score and KBSI 541 
Based on site -level annual mean values for the 80 monitoring sites, KBSI showed a 542 
strong negative relationship with the PCA -derived PC1 sediment-condition gradient (Fig. 5).  543 
Because PC1 was derived from mutually correlated sediment variables, including TAN, A VS, 544 
TOC, Metals Tier II Index, and mud fraction, it summarized the dominant field gradient 545 
represented by the measured sediment varia bles. Ordination-based approaches are commonly 546 
used in aquatic ecology to extract synthetic environmental gradients and relate them to 547 
biological assemblage patterns (ter Braak and Verdonschot, 1995). Similar gradient-based logic 548 
has also been applied in macroinvertebrate index development, where PCA of abiotic variables 549 
was used to define a disturbance gradient against which biological condition was evaluated 550 
(Blocksom and Johnson, 2009). 551 
KBSI decreased as PC1 scores increased. Under the sign convention used in this study, 552 
PC1 scores represented stronger sediment -associated environmental stress, including 553 

--- Page 26 ---

nitrogenous enrichment, reducing conditions, organic matter accumulation, relative sediment-554 
bound metal contamination, and mud deposition.  The negative PC1 –KBSI relationship 555 
therefore indicates that the final community-level index retained the intended directionality of 556 
the PC1 gradient after taxon -specific tolerance values and indicator weights were integrated 557 
across assemblages. 558 
The PC1–KBSI relationship remained significant after excluding four sites outside the 559 
95% prediction interval in the site-level annual mean analysis. This trimming step was not used 560 
to redefine KBSI or to exclude sites from the index framework. Rather, it was used to examine 561 
whether the PC1 –KBSI relationship was driven by a small number of extreme observations. 562 
After trimming, the remaining 76 sites still showed a clear negative relationship, indicating that 563 
the association between KBSI and PC1 was not dependent only on outliers. This interpretation 564 
is consistent with ecological assessment studies that emphasize the need to consider metric 565 
uncertainty a nd site -specific variability when detecting biological responses along 566 
environmental gradients (Johnson et al., 2006). 567 
The PC1 –KBSI relationship complements the relationships between KBSI and 568 
individual sediment variables described in Section 3.4. Individual variables describe specific 569 
sediment attributes, whereas PC1 summarizes their combined field covariation. PC A-based 570 
composite approaches can reduce correlated environmental variables into interpretable 571 
summary gradients; however, such gradients should be interpreted as integrated field 572 
conditions rather than as direct causal mechanisms  (Praus, 2019). Therefore, the PC1–KBSI 573 
relationship supports the internal coherence of KBSI with the sediment-condition gradient used 574 
for its development.  However, this result should not be interpreted as external validation of 575 
KBSI. Because PC1 was also used to derive the taxon -specific tolerance values and indicator 576 
weights, the relationship between PC1 and KBSI represents an internal consistency check 577 

--- Page 27 ---

rather than an independent validation test.  578

3.6. Observed–calculated KBSI and internal agreement 580 
Observed KBSI values showed a posit ive relationship with calculated KBSI values 581 
(Fig. 6). After excluding four sites that fell outside the 95% prediction interval in the preceding 582 
site-level PC1 –KBSI analysis, the remaining 76 sites retained a clear observed –calculated 583 
relationship. This result indicates that the fitted sediment-variable model reproduced the overall 584 
variation in site-level KBSI within the development dataset. 585 
Because the calculated values were derived within the same sediment -variable and 586 
index-development framework, the obs erved–calculated relationship should be interpreted as 587 
an internal agreement check rather than as evidence of predictive performance or external 588 
validation. Nevertheless, the agreement supports the internal coherence of KBSI as a biological 589 
indicator associated with the field-derived sediment-condition gradient. KBSI should therefore 590 
be interpreted together with sediment chemistry and habitat information, rather than as a stand-591 
alone measure of sediment contamination or toxicity. 592

3.7. Limitations and future validation 594 
Water-column variables, including dissolved oxygen, conductivity, nutrients, and 595 
temperature, were not measured consistently and concurrently across all site -survey sample 596 
units. Standardized hydromorphological and habitat variables were also unavailable for the 597 
complete dataset. Consequently, the independent and shared contributions of sediment 598 
condition, water -column quality, and habitat setting could not be separated statistically. 599 
Because macroinvertebrate assemblages may respond non -specifically to interacting 600 

--- Page 28 ---

environmental stressors, the observed biological response along PC1 does not by itself isolate 601 
causal sediment effects from co-varying water-column or habitat conditions (Heß et al., 2024). 602 
KBSI should therefore be interpreted as an integrated biological response associated with 603 
sediment conditions under field settings rather than as a sediment-specific measure of toxicity. 604 
Future studies should incorporate co -located water -column and hydromorphological 605 
measurements to quantify their relative contributions. 606 
The sampling framework was specifically designed for depositional sediment habitats 607 
in which fine particles and associated organic or reduced materials accumulate. Accordingly, 608 
the applicability of KBSI to coarse riffle, gravel -, or boulder -dominated habitats was not 609 
evaluated and should not be assumed. Biolo gical samples were collected to a depth of 20 cm 610 
to include deeper-burrowing taxa, whereas sediment physicochemical variables were measured 611 
in the upper 0–7 cm layer representing the biologically active surface sediment. Further 612 
validation is needed to determine whether KBSI responses remain consistent across contrasting 613 
depositional environments and waterbody types. 614 
The same monitoring dataset was used to derive taxon -specific tolerance values and 615 
indicator weights and to evaluate the relationships of KBSI  with sediment variables and the 616 
PC1 gradient. Consequently, the reported relationships represent internal consistency within 617 
the index-development dataset rather than independent validation, and their apparent strength 618 
may be optimistic. No geographically  or temporally independent dataset collected using the 619 
same sampling and taxonomic protocols was available for external validation. Seasonal effects 620 
were also not evaluated separately because the repeated surveys were not designed as a 621 
balanced seasonal ex periment. Independent validation using additional sites, temporally 622 
separate surveys, and seasonally balanced monitoring data is therefore needed to evaluate the 623 
predictive accuracy, temporal stability, and transferability of KBSI. 624 

--- Page 29 ---

Overall, KBSI provides a  field-based biological indicator of sediment -associated 625 
ecological condition in Korean freshwater systems by integrating taxon -specific tolerance 626 
values, indicator weights, and community composition along a field-derived sediment gradient. 627 
Nevertheless, it should be interpreted together with sediment chemistry and habitat information 628 
rather than as a stand-alone measure of sediment contamination or toxicity. 629

4. Conclusions 631 
This study developed the Korean Benthic Macroinvertebrate Sediment Index (KBSI) 632 
as a field -based biological indicator of sediment -associated ecological condition in Korean 633 
freshwater systems. The index was derived from a PCA -based sediment gradient integrating 634 
porewater TAN, A VS, TOC, the Metals Tier II Index, and mud fraction. Occurrence patterns of 635 
137 indicator taxa across nine PC1 class intervals were used to derive taxon-specific tolerance 636 
values and indicator weights. 637 
KBSI decreased consistently with increasing TAN, A VS, TOC, relative sediment -638 
bound metal contamination, mud fraction, and the integrated PC1 gradient. These relationships 639 
demonstrate that KBSI retained the intended biological directionality of the sediment-condition 640 
gradient within the development dataset. The observed –calculated relationship further 641 
supported internal agreement of the index formulation, although it did not constitute 642 
independent validation. 643 
KBSI is intended for biological assessment of depositional sediment habitats and 644 
should not be interpreted as an independent chemical sediment -quality standard or di rect 645 
toxicological endpoint. Its results should be considered together with sediment 646 
physicochemical and habitat information. Independent validation across additional sites, 647 

--- Page 30 ---

survey periods, and contrasting depositional environments is required to establish  its broader 648 
robustness and transferability. 649

Abstract 16 
Freshwater sediments accumulate organic matter, nutrients, metals, and fine particles and can 17 
influence benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages. However, biological tools for evaluating 18 
sediment-associated ecological condition remain limited in Korean freshw ater systems. This 19 
study developed the Korean Benthic Macroinvertebrate Sediment Index (KBSI) as a field -20 
based biological indicator using data from 80 sites and 320 site-survey sample units. Sediment 21 
variables, including porewater total ammonia nitrogen (T AN), acid-volatile sulfides (AVS), 22 
total organic carbon (TOC), the Metals Tier II Index, and mud fraction, were summarized using 23 
principal component analysis. PC1 represented an integrated sediment -condition gradient 24 
associated with organic enrichment, red ucing conditions, relative sediment -bound metal 25 
contamination, and fine-particle accumulation. PC1 scores from 309 sample units containing 26 
selected indicator taxa were divided into nine class intervals using a Weibull cumulative 27 
distribution function and Sturges’ rule. Taxon-specific tolerance values and indicator weights 28 
were derived from occurrence distributions along this gradient and used to calculate KBSI. 29 
KBSI showed significant negative relationships with TAN, AVS, TOC, the Metals Tier II 30 
Index, mud fraction, and PC1, indicating that lower index values were associated with more 31 
degraded sediment -associated field conditions. The relationship between observed and 32 
calculated KBSI values supported internal agreement within the development dataset but did 33 
not constitute independent validation. KBSI values were classified into five ecological 34 
condition classes from A to E for practical interpretation. KBSI is intended for biological 35 
assessment of depositional sediment habitats and should be interpreted together with sediment 36 
physicochemical and habitat information rather than as an independent chemical sediment -37 
quality standard or direct toxicological endpoint. 38

1. Introduction 42 
Sediment in rivers and lakes is a particulate matrix of gravel, sand, silt, clay, and 43 
organic matter that stores watershed -derived pollutants over extended periods and exchanges 44 
solutes with the water column as a dynamic reactive system (Chapman, 1990; Zhou et al., 2024). 45 
Settling and resuspension, sorption and desorption, and redox -mediated transformations 46 
continually modify sediment properties (El-Sharkawy et al., 2025; Hiki et al., 2023) . Where 47 
fine fractions dominate, contaminant accumulation and bioavailability increase and can 48 
restructure benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages (Relyea et al., 2012; Wharton et al., 2023). 49 
Therefore, single -time measurements of water -column concentrations do not adequately 50 
represent cumulative contamination or habitat alteration, underscoring the need for biological 51 
indicators that reflect sediment-associated ecological conditions. 52 
The Oligochaete Index of Sediment Bioindication (IOBS) uses oligochaete 53 
assemblage composition to evaluate sediment -associated biological condition in relation to 54 
sediment impairment (AFNOR, 2002; AFNOR, 2016), and the U.S. Equilibrium Partitioning 55 
Sediment Benchmarks (ESB) quantify linkages between sediment chemistry and biological 56 
effects (U.S.EPA, 2003). The Sediment Quali ty Triad (SQT) integrates chemistry, toxicity 57 
testing, and community data to diagnose ecological degradation in contaminated sediments 58 
(Chapman, 1990). Stress-specific biotic indices have also been developed: the Fine Sediment 59 
Biotic Index (FSBI) encodes sensitivities to fine particles (Relyea et al., 2012) , and the 60 
Proportion of Sediment-sensitive Invertebrates (PSI) uses the share of sensitive taxa to evaluate 61 
restoration outcomes (Extence et al., 2010). 62 
In coastal and estuarine environments, the Benthic Response Index (BRI) and the 63 
AZTI Marine Biotic Index (AMBI) apply weighted -tolerance principles and have informed 64 
analogous approaches for fresh waters (Borja et al., 2000; Smith et al., 2001). More recently, 65 

--- Page 40 ---

multivariate formulations—such as the Hellenic Lake Littoral Biotic Index (HeLLBI) and the 66 
Cumulative Biotic I ndex (CBI) —have related community structure to pollution gradients 67 
within explicit statistical frameworks, while high -throughput metabarcoding is improving 68 
congruence with morphology-based indices (Lobo et al., 2017; Mavromati et al., 2021; Sudarso 69 
et al., 2026). 70 
In Korea, nationally standardized tools for water -column conditions —such as the 71 
Korean Benthic macroinvertebrate Index of Biotic Integrity (KB -IBI)—are established, but 72 
sediment-focused indicators remain comparatively underdeveloped (Jun et al., 2012) . A 73 
domestic prototype, the Benthic macroinvertebrate Sediment Index (BSI), estimated taxon 74 
tolerance values and indicator weights from relative frequencies in fr eshwater sediments and 75 
computed a weighted mean using a Sediment Quality Index that integrates total organic carbon 76 
(TOC), ammonia (NH ₃–N), and heavy metals (Kim et al., 2019) . Although BSI showed 77 
statistical associations with sediment impairment gradients, its generality was constrained by 78 
limited sample size and taxonomic uncertainty within Chironomidae, indicating the need for a 79 
tool calibrated with broader spatial coverage and refined taxonomy. 80 
To address this gap, we developed the Korean Benthic Macroinvertebrate Sediment 81 
Index (KBSI) as a field-based biological indicator of sediment-associated ecological condition 82 
in Korean freshwaters. Specifically, this study aimed to: (1) assemble a nationwi de dataset of 83 
co-located benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages and sediment physicochemical conditions 84 
from major Korean river basins and representative waterbody types; (2) define an integrated 85 
sediment environmental gradient using PCA of porewater total ammonia nitrogen, acid-volatile 86 
sulfides, total organic carbon, heavy metals, and the mud fraction; (3) derive taxon -specific 87 
tolerance values and indicator weights from macroinvertebrate occurrence patterns along the 88 
PC1-based sediment gradient; and (4) f ormulate and examine KBSI as a community -level 89 

--- Page 41 ---

index by relating index values to individual sediment variables, the PC1 sediment gradient, and 90 
observed–calculated index agreement. KBSI is therefore intended not as a stand -alone 91 
chemical or toxicological measure of sediment quality, but as a biological indicator of benthic 92 
assemblage responses to sediment-associated environmental conditions under field settings. 93

2. Materials and methods 95 
2.1 Study area and sampling design 96 
2.1.1 Site description  97 
This study was conducted at 80 freshwater sediment monitoring sites distributed across 98 
the major river basins of Korea (Fig. 1). The sites were selected to represent broad gradients in 99 
sedimentary environments, waterbody type, and anthropogenic influence, including both lotic 100 
and lentic systems. For descriptive and management -oriented interpretation, the sites were 101 
categorized into five groups: general streams, urban streams, industrial streams, lakes, and 102 
reservoirs. The stream categories were assigned acc ording to the dominant surrounding land -103 
use setting and the original monitoring-site designation, rather than using uniform quantitative 104 
catchment-scale land -use thresholds. Urban streams represented lotic sites located in 105 
predominantly urbanized settings, whereas industrial streams represented sites associated with 106 
industrial areas. General streams comprised the remaining lotic sites that were not classified as 107 
urban or industrial streams. 108 
These categories were used to describe broad differences in waterbo dy type, 109 
surrounding land -use setting, and sediment depositional environment, rather than to define 110 
strict reference or impairment classes. General streams were therefore not treated as reference 111 
or best -available sites and could include streams influenced  by agriculture, forestry, flow 112 

--- Page 42 ---

regulation, impoundments, or other catchment and hydromorphological pressures. 113 
Field surveys were conducted repeatedly between 2022 and 2024, producing a total of 114 
320 site-survey sample units. For the derivation of taxon-specific tolerance values and indicator 115 
weights, 309 sample units in which selected indicator taxa occurred were used. Sample units 116 
without selected indicator taxa were excluded from the tolerance -value calculation because 117 
they did not provide the taxon-occurrence information required to estimate biological responses 118 
along the sediment environmental gradient. 119 
The sampling design was developed to support a field-based biological assessment of 120 
sediment-associated ecological condition. Accordingly, biological and sediment samples were 121 
collected from comparable depositional habitats within each site, where fine sediments and 122 
associated organic or reduced materials could accumulate. The sampling framework was 123 
therefore intended for depositional sediment habitats, not for coarse riffle or boulder-dominated 124 
substrates. This design allowed the KBSI to characterize benthic macroinvertebrate assemblage 125 
responses to sediment-associated environmental conditions under field settings. 126 
 127 
2.1.2. Collection and Identification of Benthic Macroinvertebrates 128 
Benthic macroinvertebrates were collected from depositional sediment habitats using 129 
a cylindrical core sampler with an internal diameter of 7.5 cm. At each site, five replicate cores 130 
were taken to a depth of 20 cm where coring was feasible (Kim et al., 2018; Song et al., 2025). 131 
The 20 cm sampling depth was adopted to capture not only organisms concentrated in the upper 132 
sediment layer, but also burrowing taxa, particularly oligochaetes, that may occur in deeper 133 
sediment layers. 134 
In the field, sediment core contents were washed through a 0.20-mm sieve to minimize 135 

--- Page 43 ---

the loss of small -bodied sediment -dwelling organisms, particularly oligochaetes and 136 
chironomid larvae. Retained materials were preserved in ethanol and transported to the 137 
laboratory. The use of a 0.20 -mm sieve was selected because small -bodied taxa in fine 138 
sediments can be underestimated when larger mesh sizes are used (Song et al., 2025). 139 
In the laboratory, residual sediment was removed and organisms were sorted under a 140 
stereomicroscope. Specimens were identified to the lowest practicable tax onomic level, 141 
generally to species where diagnostic characters were available, using standard taxonomic 142 
references (Cummins and Merritt, 1996; Kawai and Tanida, 2005; Peckarsky et al., 1990; Yoon, 143 
1988). Chironomid larvae were identified based on diagnostic structures such as the head 144 
capsule, mentum, mandible, and antennae (Kwak, 2015). Oligochaetes were identified using 145 
external and reproductive morphological characters, including chaetal and reproductive 146 
structures (Park et al., 2016) . All identified specimens were preserved in 80% ethanol to 147 
minimize morphological deformation.  148 
Taxa that were sufficiently frequent and taxonomically reliable were retained as 149 
candidate indicator taxa for KBSI development. This taxonomic screening was necessary 150 
because the derivation of tolerance values and indicator weights requires reliable occurrence 151 
information along the sediment environmental gradient. 152

2.1.3. Sediment Sampling and Physicochemical Analysis 154 
Surface sediment samples for physicochemical analysis were collected concurrently 155 
with the biological samples from the corresponding depositional habitats within each site using 156 
a cylindrical core sampler with an inter nal diameter of 7.5 cm.  For sediment chemistry and 157 
particle-size analysis, the upper 0–7 cm sediment layer was used because this layer represents 158 

--- Page 44 ---

the biologically active surface sediment where organic matter accumulation, redox gradients, 159 
porewater chemist ry, and contaminant binding are most pronounced. This analytical depth 160 
differed from the macroinvertebrate sampling depth of 20 cm, which was used to include 161 
deeper-burrowing taxa. 162 
Each sediment sample was sealed in a 300-mL high-density polyethylene bottle, stored 163 
at ≤4 °C, and transported to the laboratory. The target sediment variables were porewater total 164

metals, and particle -size composition. TAN was used as an indi cator of nitrogenous organic 166 
enrichment in sediment porewater, A VS represented reduced sediment conditions associated 167

2.2. Development and calculation of the Korea n Benthic Macroinvertebrate Sediment Index 189 
(KBSI) 190 
2.2.1. PCA-derived sediment environmental gradient 191 
To derive an integrated sediment environmental gradient for KBSI development, 192 
principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the sediment physicochemical 193 
variables measured at each sample unit. PCA was used to reduce redundancy among correlated 194 
environmental variables and to summarize the dominant multivariate gradient in sediment 195 
condition (Abdi and Williams, 2010; Jolliffe and Cadima, 2016) . The variables included 196 
porewater total ammonia nitrogen (TAN), acid -volatile sulfides (A VS), total organic carbon 197 
(TOC), the Metals Tier II Index, and the mud fraction. These variables were selected to 198 
represent major sediment -associated conditions, includ ing nitrogenous organic enrichment, 199 
reduced sediment conditions, organic matter accumulation, relative sediment -bound metal 200 
contamination, and fine-particle deposition. 201 
Prior to PCA, variables with skewed distributions were transformed to reduce the 202 
influence of extreme values. TAN, A VS, TOC, and the Metals Tier II Index were log -203 
transformed, whereas the mud fraction, expressed as proportional data, was arcsine square-root 204 

--- Page 46 ---

transformed. All transformed variables were standardized to a mean of zero and a stan dard 205 
deviation of one before analysis, and PCA was conducted on the standardized environmental 206 
matrix (Legendre and Legendre, 2012; Quinn and Keough, 2002). 207 
The first principal component score was used as the integrated sediment environmental 208 
gradient for deriving taxon-specific tolerance values and indicator weights. PC1 was selected 209 
because it represented the dominant axis of variation in sediment conditions and provided a 210 
continuous gradient along which macroinvertebrate occurrence patterns could be evaluated. In 211 
this study, PC1 was not interpreted as a stand-alone chemical pollution index or an independent 212 
measure of sediment toxicity. Instead, it was used as a multivariate sediment-condition gradient 213 
reflecting field-based co-variation among sediment physicochemical variables. 214 
The PC1 scores of sample units containing selected indicator taxa were used in the 215 
subsequent derivation of biological indicator values. Among the 320 site-survey sample units, 216 
309 sample units with selected indicator taxa were retained for this step, whereas sample units 217 
without selected indicator taxa were excluded because they did not provide occurrence 218 
information required for estimating taxon responses along the sediment environmental gradient. 219

3. Results and discussion 323 
3.1. PCA-derived sediment environmental gradient 324 
Principal component analysis summarized the covariance structure of the five 325 
sediment physicochemical variables used to characterize sediment-associated field conditions: 326 
porewater total ammonia nitrogen (TAN), acid -volatile sulfides (A VS), total organic carbon 327 
(TOC), Metals Tier II Index, and mud fraction. The first three PCA axes explained 86.0% of 328 
the total variance, with PC1 accounting for 58.0%, PC2 for 17.2%, and PC3 for 10.8% (Table 329 
1; Fig. 2). PC1 was the dominant axis because all five sediment vari ables showed clear 330 
associations with this axis. The loadings on PC1 were 0.66 for ln(TAN), 0.69 for ln(A VS), 0.86 331 
for ln(TOC), 0.82 for ln(Metals Tier II Index), and 0.76 for ln(mud fraction).  Thus, PC1 332 
represented a composite sediment environmental gradient along which nitrogenous enrichment, 333 
reducing sediment conditions, organic matter accumulation, relative sediment -bound metal 334 
contamination, and fine-particle deposition co-varied. 335 
Organic matter accumulation is ecologically relevant because it can incre ase oxygen 336 
demand in surface sediments and promote conditions unfavorable to oxygen-sensitive benthic 337 
fauna (Rosenberg et al., 2001). A VS reflects reduced sediment conditions, and sulfide-rich or 338 
strongly reduced sediments can  modify metal binding, porewater chemistry, and exposure 339 

--- Page 52 ---

conditions for sediment-dwelling organisms (Simpson et al., 2013). TAN was interpreted as a 340 
porewater indicator of nitrogenous organic enrichment rather than as a stand -alone toxicant 341 
gradient, because ammonia effects in sediments depend on pH, redox condition, and 342 
porewater–overlying water exchange. The Metals Tier II Index summarized the relative degree 343 
of sediment -bound metal contamination, but its ecological interpretation should consider 344 
bioavailability and site -specific geochemical conditions rather than total concentration alon e 345 
(Simpson et al., 2013). Therefore, the strong contribution of TOC, A VS, TAN, and the Metals 346 
Tier II Index indicates that PC1 captured a biogeochemical gradient associated with organic 347 
enrichment, reduced sediment conditions, and relative sediment-bound metal contamination. 348 
Mud fraction also contributed strongly to PC1, but its interpreta tion differs from that 349 
of chemical contaminants. Fine particles can reduce interstitial flow, alter oxygen exchange, 350 
and modify benthic habitat structure in lotic environments (Wood and Armitage, 1997). Fine-351 
grained sediment can also affect macroinvertebrate assemblages by filling substrate interstices 352 
and reducing the availability of coarse-substrate microhabitats used by rheophilic or sediment-353 
sensitive taxa (Bylak and Kukuła, 2022). In addition, fine sediments can enhance the retention 354 
of organic matter and particle -reactive contaminants because of their la rge surface area and 355 
depositional stability (Wharton et al., 2023) . However, mud accumulation may arise from 356 
natural hydromorphological and depositional processes as well as from anthropogenic changes 357 
in flow regulation, land use, and sediment supply (Wharton et al., 2023; Wood and Armitage, 358 
1997). Accordingly, mud fraction was treated as a physical depositional variable rather than as 359 
a chemical contaminant. 360 
For these reasons, PC1 was interpreted as a field -based multivariate sediment -361 
condition gradient, not as a stand -alone sediment toxicity index or a regulatory sediment -362 
quality standard. Sediment chemistry and benthic community composition can provide 363 

--- Page 53 ---

complementary lines of evidence for sediment -associated ecological assessment, but neither 364 
line of evidence alone fully resolves causal mechanisms under field conditions (Long and 365 
Chapman, 1985). The sediment quality triad framework similarly emphasizes that sediment 366 
chemistry, toxicity, and benthic community responses should be interpreted together rather than 367 
treated as interchangeable measures (Chapman, 1990). In the present study, KBSI was derived 368 
from macroinvertebrate occurrence patterns along the PC1 gradient; therefore, it should be 369 
interpreted as a biological indicator of sediment -associated ecological condition under field 370 
settings, not as an independent chemical or toxicological assessment of sediment quality. 371

3.2. Classification of PC1 scores and derivation of class values 373 
Among the 320 site-survey sample units, 309 sample units contained selected indicator 374 
taxa and were therefore used to classify the PC1 sediment -condition gradient. The remaining 375 
11 sample units were excluded from this step because they did not contain selected indicator 376 
taxa and therefore provided no taxon -occurrence information for deriving tolerance values or 377 
indicator weights. The PC1 scores of the 309 retained sample units were used to des cribe the 378 
empirical distribution of sediment -associated field conditions represented by the indicator -379 
taxon dataset. 380 
The distribution of PC1 scores was fitted using a Weibull cumulative distribution 381 
function (Fig. 3). The Weibull model was used to describe the probability distribution of PC1 382 
scores and to derive class boundaries along the continuous sediment -condition gradient. 383 
Because the objective was to construct empirical intervals for biological indicator derivation 384 
rather than to define regulatory sed iment-quality thresholds, the distribution was divided into 385 
equal cumulative-probability intervals. Sturges’ rule produced nine class intervals for the 309 386 
sample units, and the inverse Weibull cumulative distribution function was used to estimate the 387 

--- Page 54 ---

PC1 boundary and representative class value for each interval (Table 2). 388 
The resulting PC1 classes covered the observed sediment-condition gradient from low 389 
PC1 scores to high PC1 scores. Under the sign convention used in this study, higher PC1 scores 390 
represented stronger sediment -associated environmental stress, including greater organic 391 
enrichment, reducing conditions, relative sediment -bound metal contamination, and mud 392 
accumulation. The representative class values therefore provided a standardized n umerical 393 
basis for estimating taxon-specific occurrence positions along the sediment-condition gradient. 394 
Taxa occurring mainly in lower PC1 classes were assigned lower tolerance values, whereas 395 
taxa occurring mainly in higher PC1 classes were assigned higher tolerance values. 396 
Importantly, these PC1 classes should not be interpreted as sediment-quality standards 397 
or toxicity thresholds. They are empirical intervals of a field -based multivariate sediment -398 
condition gradient derived from the distribution of PC1 scores in sample units containing 399 
indicator taxa. This distinction is necessary because PC1 integrates chemical variables with 400 
mud fraction, a physical depositional variable that can reflect both natural hydromorphological 401 
processes and anthropogenic sediment accumulation. The class values were therefore used only 402 
as an internal scaling framework for deriving KBSI tolerance values and indicator weights, not 403 
as independent criteria for classifying sediment contamination or toxicity. 404 
 405 
3.3. Taxon-specific tolerance values and indicator weights 406 
Taxon-specific tolerance values and indicator weights were derived from the 407 
occurrence distributions of selected indicator taxa across the nine PC1 class intervals (Table 3). 408 
In total, 137 indicator taxa were retained in the final indicator table. This approach follows the 409 
logic of field-based macroinvertebrate bioassessment, in which taxon scores are inferred from 410 

--- Page 55 ---

observed distributions along environmental gradients rather than from a sing le chemical 411 
variable or laboratory endpoint (Hering et al., 2004; Rosenberg and Resh, 1993). It also builds 412 
on the saprobic valency concept and its later adaptations, where taxa are characterized by both 413 
their position along an environmental gradient and their strength of  indication within that 414 
gradient (Sládeček, 1967; Yoon et al., 1992; Zelinka and Marvan, 1961). 415 
Although KBSI shares a weighted -indicator structure with the Korean BMI  (Kong et 416 
al., 2018) and conventional saprobic indices, the indices differ in their target environmental  417 
gradients and application domains. BMI is used primarily to assess the general ecological 418 
condition of Korean streams, whereas KBSI was derived from depositional sediment habitats 419 
in both lotic and lentic systems. Similarly, conventional saprobic values p rimarily represent 420 
taxon distributions along gradients of organic pollution and oxygen conditions, whereas KBSI 421 
tolerance values were independently derived along a multivariate sediment-condition gradient 422 
integrating TAN, A VS, TOC, relative metal contamination, and mud fraction. Correspondence 423 
among taxon classifications may therefore reflect shared responses to organic enrichment and 424 
oxygen depletion, but KBSI tolerance values should not be interpreted as conventional saprobic 425 
values. 426 
Tolerance values represent the weighted average position of each taxon along the PC1 427 
sediment-condition gradient. Lower values indicate taxa whose occurrences were concentrated 428 
in lower PC1 classes, whereas higher values indicate taxa whose occurrences were 429 
concentrated in higher PC1 classes. Under the sign convention used in this study, higher PC1 430 
scores represented stronger sediment -associated environmental stress, including greater 431 
organic enrichment, reducing conditions, metal contamination, and mud accumulation. 432 
Accordingly, tolerance values should be interpreted as field-derived occurrence positions along 433 
the measured PC1 gradient, not as fixed physiological toxicity thresholds. 434 

--- Page 56 ---

The resulting values showed broad taxon -level variation. Taxa with low tolerance 435 
values included Rhyacodrilus coccineus (t = 0.0), Chaetogaster limnaei (t = 0.0), Limnodrilus 436 
claparedeianus (t = 0.7), and Chaetogaster diaphanus  (t = 1.9), whereas taxa with higher 437 
tolerance values included Limnodrilus udekemianus (t = 7.3) and Branchiodrilus hortensis (t = 438 
7.1). These differences are consistent with the general observation that benthic 439 
macroinvertebrate assemblages vary along gradients of organic enrichment, oxygen depletion, 440 
sediment condition, and habitat disturbance (Townsend et al., 2008; V on Bertrab et al., 2013). 441 
However, the values should not be over -interpreted as universal taxon traits beca use field 442 
distributions can also reflect habitat structure, flow regime, sampling depth, and regional 443 
species pools. 444 
Indicator weights describe a different property from tolerance values. A high indicator 445 
weight means that a taxon ’s occurrences were concen trated within a restricted portion of the 446 
PC1 gradient, whereas a low weight indicates a broader distribution across classes. Thus, 447 
indicator weight represents occurrence specificity rather than sensitivity or tolerance itself. 448 
This distinction follows the  weighted-indicator logic of saprobic and Korean benthic 449 
macroinvertebrate indices, where community-level scores combine taxon position, abundance 450 
or occurrence, and indicator strength (Zelinka & Marvan, 1961; Yoon et al., 1992; Kong & 451 
Kim, 2016; Kim et al., 2019). 452 
This distinction also resolves the issue of taxa with tolerance values of 0.0. In the final 453 
table, Rhyacodrilus coccineus  and Chaetogaster limnaei  had tolerance values of 0.0 and 454 
indicator weights of 5, because their occurrences were concentrated  in the lowest PC1 class. 455 
Their high indicator weights therefore reflect narrow occurrence specificity, not an automatic 456 
assignment based on sensitivity. Conversely, taxa with intermediate or high tolerance values 457 
may receive low weights if they are broadl y distributed across PC1 classes. This treatment 458 

--- Page 57 ---

prevents tolerance value and indicator weight from being conflated into a single sensitivity 459 
metric. 460 
Overall, Table 3 should be interpreted as a field -derived indicator table for sediment -461 
associated ecologic al condition in the sampled Korean freshwater systems. The table 462 
summarizes how selected taxa were distributed along the measured PC1 gradient, but it does 463 
not imply that KBSI fully isolates sediment effects from water -column conditions or habitat 464 
context. This cautious interpretation is consistent with macroinvertebrate bioassessment 465 
frameworks, which recognize that benthic taxa integrate multiple stressors and habitat -scale 466 
processes rather than responding to a single environmental factor alone (Birk et al., 2012; 467 
Hering et al., 2004; Rosenberg and Resh, 1993) . The use of taxon -specific tolerance values 468 
requires standardized identification at the lowest practicable taxonomic level and may limit 469 
routine application where specialist taxonomic expertise is unavailable. Future operational 470 
evaluation should determ ine whether selected taxa can be aggregated to higher taxonomic 471 
levels without materially reducing the discriminatory performance of KBSI. 472

3.4. Relationship between KBSI and sediment environmental variables 474 
Using site -level annual mean values for the 80 monitoring sites, KBSI showed 475 
consistent negative relationships with the five sediment environmental variables used to define 476 
the PC1 sediment-condition gradient (Fig. 4). KBSI decreased with increasing porewater TAN, 477 
A VS, TOC, Metals Tier II Index, and mu d fraction, and all relationships were statistically 478 
significant. At the site level, the correlation coefficients were −0.57 for TAN, −0.60 for A VS, 479 
−0.65 for TOC, −0.64 for the Metals Tier II Index, and −0.51 for mud fraction.  Thus, the 480 
strongest associations were observed for TOC and Metals Tier II Index, followed by A VS, TAN, 481 
and mud fraction. These results indicate that lower KBSI values were associated with 482 

--- Page 58 ---

depositional sediments characterized by greater organic matter content, greater relative 483 
sediment-bound metal contamination, stronger reducing conditions, higher porewater nitrogen 484 
enrichment, and greater fine-particle accumulation. 485 
The negative relationship between KBSI and TOC suggests that the index responded 486 
to organic matter accumulation in surf ace sediments. Organic matter enrichment can increase 487 
microbial oxygen demand and promote low-oxygen or reducing conditions in benthic habitats, 488 
thereby affecting sediment-dwelling fauna and benthic community composition (Eggleton and 489 
Thomas, 2004; Rosenberg et al., 2001) . TOC can also interact with redox condition and 490 
contaminant binding, so its relationship with KBSI should be interpreted as part of a broader 491 
sediment biogeochemical gradient rather than as an isolated single-stressor effect (Zhang et al., 492 
2014). 493 
KBSI also decreased with incre asing TAN. In this study, TAN was interpreted as a 494 
porewater indicator of nitrogenous organic enrichment rather than as a stand -alone toxicant 495 
gradient. Ammonia and other inorganic nitrogen forms can affect aquatic organisms, but their 496 
effects depend on ch emical speciation, pH, temperature, oxygen condition, and exchange 497 
between porewater and overlying water (Camargo and Alonso, 2006; U.S. EPA, 2013) . 498 
Therefore, the KBSI –TAN relationship is best interpreted as a field -level association with 499 
nitrogenous enrichment and porewater condition within depositional sediments. 500 
The negative relationship between KBSI and A VS indicates that lower index values 501 
were associated with more reduced sediment conditions. A VS is important in sediment 502 
assessment because sulfide-rich conditions reflect reducing environments and can modify both 503 
porewater chemistry and metal partitioning (Di Toro et al., 1992) . However, A VS should not 504 
be interpreted simply as a direct stressor with a single directional effect, because sulfides can 505 
bind divalent metals and reduce their bioavailability under some geochemical conditions (Di 506 

--- Page 59 ---

Toro et al., 1992; Zhang et al., 2014) . Accordingly, the KBSI –A VS relationship should be 507 
interpreted as a biological response associated with reduced, organic -rich depositional 508 
conditions rather than as direct evidence of sulfide toxicity. 509 
KBSI decreased with increasing Metals Tier II Index, suggesting that assemblages with 510 
lower KBSI values tended to occur where relative sediment -bound metal contamination was 511 
higher. Sediments can act as both sinks and secondary sources of metals, and metal mobility 512 
and bioavailability are influenced by particle size, organic matter, Fe –Mn oxides, A VS, pH, 513 
redox condition, and biological activity (Eggleton and Thomas, 2004; Förstner and Wittmann, 514 
1981). Therefore, the relationship between KBSI and the Metals Tier II Index should be 515 
interpreted as a field -level association between benthic assemblage condition and relative 516 
sediment-bound metal contamination, not as a direct measurement of metal toxicity.  This 517 
distinction is important because total or guideline -normalized metal concentrations do not 518 
necessarily represent the fraction that is bioavailable to benthic organisms (Di Toro et al., 1992; 519 
Zhang et al., 2014). 520 
KBSI also showed a statistically significant but moderate negative relationship with 521 
mud fraction, indicating a general tendency for lower KBSI values to occur in depositional 522 
sediments with a greater silt–clay proportion. However, mud fraction alone accounted for only 523 
a limited proportion of the variation in KBSI and should not be interpreted as the dominant 524 
determinant of index values. Fine sediments can alter benthic habitats by reducing interstitial 525 
flow, filling substrate spaces, changing oxygen ex change, and increasing the retention of 526 
organic matter and particle -reactive contaminants (V on Bertrab et al., 2013; Wood and 527 
Armitage, 1997). Fine-sediment deposition can also influence macroinvertebrate assemblage 528 
composition and interact with other stressors such as organic enrichment and water -quality 529 
degradation (Doretto et al., 2018; Extence et al., 2010) . Nevertheless, mud fraction is not a 530 

--- Page 60 ---

chemical contaminant. It can reflect natural depositional and hydromorphological processes as 531 
well as anthropogenic changes in flow regulation, sediment supply, land use, or channel 532 
modification (Wharton et al., 2023; Wood and Armitage, 1997). 533 
Overall, these relationships support the internal consistency of KBSI with the sediment 534 
variables used to define the PC1 gradient. They indicate that KBSI varied consistently along 535 
field gradients involving organic enrichment, reducing conditions, relative sediment -bound 536 
metal contamination, and fine -particle deposition. However, these associations do not 537 
demonstrate that KBSI isolates sediment effects from co-varying water-column conditions or 538 
habitat characteristics (Birk et al., 2012; Feld and Hering, 2007; Townsend et al., 2008). 539

3.5. Relationship between PC1 score and KBSI 541 
Based on site -level annual mean values for the 80 monitoring sites, KBSI showed a 542 
strong negative relationship with the PCA -derived PC1 sediment-condition gradient (Fig. 5).  543 
Because PC1 was derived from mutually correlated sediment variables, including TAN, A VS, 544 
TOC, Metals Tier II Index, and mud fraction, it summarized the dominant field gradient 545 
represented by the measured sediment variables. Ordination -based approaches are commonly 546 
used in aquatic ecology to extract synthetic environmental gradients and relate them to 547 
biological assemblage patterns (ter Braak and Verdonschot, 1995). Similar gradient-based logic 548 
has also been applied in macroinvertebrate index development, where PCA of abiotic variables 549 
was used to define a disturbance gradient against which biological condition was evaluated 550 
(Blocksom and Johnson, 2009). 551 
KBSI decreased as PC1 scores increased. Under the sign convention used in this study, 552 
PC1 scores represented stronger sediment -associated environmental stress, including 553 

--- Page 61 ---

nitrogenous enrichment, reducing conditions, organic matter accumulation, relative sediment-554 
bound metal contamination, and mud deposition.  The negative PC1 –KBSI relationship 555 
therefore indicates that the final community-level index retained the intended directionality of 556 
the PC1 gradient after taxon -specific tolerance values and indicator weights were integrated 557 
across assemblages. 558 
The PC1–KBSI relationship remained significant after excluding four sites outside the 559 
95% prediction interval in the site-level annual mean analysis. This trimming step was not used 560 
to redefine KBSI or to exclude sites from the index framework. Rather, it was used to examine 561 
whether the PC1 –KBSI relationship was driven by a small number of extreme observations. 562 
After trimming, the remaining 76 sites still showed a clear negative relationship, indicating that 563 
the association between KBSI and PC1 was not dependent only on outliers. This interpretation 564 
is consistent with ecological assessment studies that emphasize the need to consider metric 565 
uncertainty a nd site -specific variability when detecting biological responses along 566 
environmental gradients (Johnson et al., 2006). 567 
The PC1 –KBSI relationship complements the relationships between KBSI and 568 
individual sediment variables described in Section 3.4. Individual variables describe specific 569 
sediment attributes, whereas PC1 summarizes their combined field covariation. PC A-based 570 
composite approaches can reduce correlated environmental variables into interpretable 571 
summary gradients; however, such gradients should be interpreted as integrated field 572 
conditions rather than as direct causal mechanisms  (Praus, 2019). Therefore, the PC1 –KBSI 573 
relationship supports the internal coherence of KBSI with the sediment-condition gradient used 574 
for its development.  However, this result should not be interpreted as external validation of 575 
KBSI. Because PC1 was also used to derive the taxon -specific tolerance values and indicator 576 
weights, the relationship between PC1 and KBSI represents an internal consistency check 577 

--- Page 62 ---

rather than an independent validation test.  578

3.6. Observed–calculated KBSI and internal agreement 580 
Observed KBSI values showed a positive relationship with calculated KBSI values 581 
(Fig. 6). After excluding four sites that fell outside the 95% prediction interval in the preceding 582 
site-level PC1 –KBSI analysis, the remaining 76 sites retained a clear observed –calculated 583 
relationship. This result indicates that the fitted sediment-variable model reproduced the overall 584 
variation in site-level KBSI within the development dataset. 585 
Because the calculated values were derived within the same sediment -variable and 586 
index-development framework, the observed–calculated relationship should be interpreted as 587 
an internal agreement check rather than as evidence of predictive performance or external 588 
validation. Nevertheless, the agreement supports the internal coherence of KBSI as a biological 589 
indicator associated with the field-derived sediment-condition gradient. KBSI should therefore 590 
be interpreted together with sediment chemistry and habitat information, rather than as a stand-591 
alone measure of sediment contamination or toxicity. 592

3.7. Limitations and future validation 594 
Water-column variables, including dissolved oxygen, conductivity, nutrients, and 595 
temperature, were not measured consistently and concurrently across all site -survey sample 596 
units. Standardized hydromorphological and habitat variables were also unav ailable for the 597 
complete dataset. Consequently, the independent and shared contributions of sediment 598 
condition, water -column quality, and habitat setting could not be separated statistically. 599 
Because macroinvertebrate assemblages may respond non -specifically to interacting 600 

--- Page 63 ---

environmental stressors, the observed biological response along PC1 does not by itself isolate 601 
causal sediment effects from co-varying water-column or habitat conditions (Heß et al., 2024). 602 
KBSI should therefore be interpreted as an integrated biological response associated with 603 
sediment conditions under field settings rather than as a sediment-specific measure of toxicity. 604 
Future studies should incorporate co -located water -column and hydromorphological 605 
measurements to quantify their relative contributions. 606 
The sampling framework was specifically designed for depositional sediment habitats 607 
in which fine particles and associated organic or reduced materials accumulate. Accordingly, 608 
the applicability of KBSI to coarse riffle, gravel -, or boulder -dominated habitats was not 609 
evaluated and should not be assumed. Biological sam ples were collected to a depth of 20 cm 610 
to include deeper-burrowing taxa, whereas sediment physicochemical variables were measured 611 
in the upper 0–7 cm layer representing the biologically active surface sediment. Further 612 
validation is needed to determine whether KBSI responses remain consistent across contrasting 613 
depositional environments and waterbody types. 614 
The same monitoring dataset was used to derive taxon -specific tolerance values and 615 
indicator weights and to evaluate the relationships of KBSI with sed iment variables and the 616 
PC1 gradient. Consequently, the reported relationships represent internal consistency within 617 
the index-development dataset rather than independent validation, and their apparent strength 618 
may be optimistic. No geographically or tempo rally independent dataset collected using the 619 
same sampling and taxonomic protocols was available for external validation. Seasonal effects 620 
were also not evaluated separately because the repeated surveys were not designed as a 621 
balanced seasonal experiment.  Independent validation using additional sites, temporally 622 
separate surveys, and seasonally balanced monitoring data is therefore needed to evaluate the 623 
predictive accuracy, temporal stability, and transferability of KBSI. 624 

--- Page 64 ---

Overall, KBSI provides a field -based biological indicator of sediment -associated 625 
ecological condition in Korean freshwater systems by integrating taxon -specific tolerance 626 
values, indicator weights, and community composition along a field-derived sediment gradient. 627 
Nevertheless, it should be interpreted together with sediment chemistry and habitat information 628 
rather than as a stand-alone measure of sediment contamination or toxicity. 629

4. Conclusions 631 
This study developed the Korean Benthic Macroinvertebrate Sediment Index (KBSI) 632 
as a field-based biological indicator of sediment -associated ecological condition in Korean 633 
freshwater systems. The index was derived from a PCA -based sediment gradient integrating 634 
porewater TAN, A VS, TOC, the Metals Tier II Index, and mud fraction. Occurrence patterns of 635 
137 indicator taxa across nine PC1 class intervals were used to derive taxon-specific tolerance 636 
values and indicator weights. 637 
KBSI decreased consistently with increasing TAN, A VS, TOC, relative sediment -638 
bound metal contamination, mud fraction, and the integrated PC1 gradient. These relationships 639 
demonstrate that KBSI retained the intended biological directionality of the sediment-condition 640 
gradient within the development dataset. The observed –calculated relationship further 641 
supported internal ag reement of the index formulation, although it did not constitute 642 
independent validation. 643 
KBSI is intended for biological assessment of depositional sediment habitats and 644 
should not be interpreted as an independent chemical sediment -quality standard or dire ct 645 
toxicological endpoint. Its results should be considered together with sediment 646 
physicochemical and habitat information. Independent validation across additional sites, 647 

--- Page 65 ---

survey periods, and contrasting depositional environments is required to establish i ts broader 648 
robustness and transferability. 649
